Now let's look at the firmware features. I'll skip the very basic stuff like uploading patterns, checking status, and WiFi setup, and start slowly with the audio side. OSC was probably the first thing I added. The reason I went with that instead of MIDI was that I didn't know how convenient MIDI was, and in a course I'd taken on making interactive content with Unreal and Ableton, we'd used OSC. MIDI is so convenient that even I don't use this anymore, but for more specific work… there'll be moments it's needed! Though it's true that setting up Max patches and all is a hassle.

MIDI is really convenient. Just install rtpMIDI, a program that lets you send MIDI signals over WiFi, and there's no other setup needed. At most you change how MIDI signals are used in the program's settings? But that's a very basic setting that nobody wouldn't know, and it's hardly a hassle. Mapping knobs to parameters is so easy too: Ctrl+M to turn on MIDI mapping, click the target parameter, turn a Patternflow knob, and it's linked. Sending from the program back to Patternflow is also simpler than with OSC. Look into that one yourself if you're curious!

That isn't to say OSC is completely useless, because it's just a protocol spec, meaning just an agreement set up for efficient communication, so once you open up the path you can use it in other forms. There's a Chrome extension. Install it in Chrome and open it and you get a really fun interface; with FFT-something you can analyze the sound by frequency band. Put simply, you can hook only the high frequencies to knob 4 and the low ones to knob 1, that kind of thing. Play a YouTube video or Spotify in Chrome, set things up a little, and you've got a pretty fun audio-synced visual.

It's much simpler than setting up tracks and MIDI signals in Ableton. There are limits to binding audio and visuals in detailed, unusual ways, though. Still, it's enough to show people, because… honestly, if it's complicated it isn't intuitive and people might wonder what they're looking at. Complex and new and yet intuitive to use would be the best!

You could do it with the Chrome extension, but you might want more: to react to real sound, and work without any separate internet connection. Then you solder a PDM microphone onto the ESP32. Nothing hard about it. It's four wires, was it, to solder at most. And since it goes on the board, it's really easy. Naturally, though, precision falls short of the earlier method that analyzes the audio output data directly. The sound comes in kind of lumped together, and it's hard to fit a good mic… hm. Anyway, let's take satisfaction in the fact that it works. As I said, intuitive is best, so even just reacting to how loud it is is fun.

MQTT is a feature for connecting multiple Patternflows. As far as I know, one host broadcasts its state to multiple subscribed devices. Honestly, I don't really know the specifics…! But this way you can connect Patternflows around the world in real time, so when someone turns a knob, other people's devices change too. When we tested it and I saw Simone turning a knob in Dubai take effect on my device in Korea, it was a strange feeling. It doesn't have to be across that kind of distance either; you could set several Patternflows in a nearby space and have them change together.

There's a sibling to this called flowlocal, which hasn't been released yet, so later you can refer to the material Simone puts together! Like how I'm using it for the upcoming exhibition, it's useful for making performances or installations run standalone, unaffected by the surrounding environment, exactly as they did in the development setup. That's how I use it! There's also a feature in it for setting up patterns and sequences like a Launchpad and playing with them, and it's really fun, so do try it someday.

That's where the .pfs (Patternflow Sequence) I mentioned earlier comes in. You fix the detailed pattern changes in place and arrange and tune the big chunks by feel. I'd love for these to be shared like DJ sets someday.
